A note on Blackboard Ally and other accessibility checkers

Blackboard Ally is the accessibility tool built into Blackboard. It scans your course materials automatically, assigns each file an accessibility score, and compiles those scores into a Course Accessibility Report for the course as a whole. The accessibility characteristics it is checking for help far more students than the ones with a formal accommodation on file: clear headings, useful alt text, and accurate captions make materials easier to follow for almost anyone, whether they're on a phone, in a loud room, or trying to search a document for one specific term.

Ally's accessibility score is most useful when viewed like feedback on a rough draft, not a grade on a final paper — it's pointing you toward specific things to review, not scoring the course, or you.

Ally, and the accessibility checkers built into Word and PowerPoint, are all automated tools. They're genuinely good at catching things that follow a clear, consistent rule: a missing label, a contrast ratio below a set number, a table with no header row. They can't tell you whether an alt text description is actually useful, whether a caption is accurate, or whether a reading order makes sense to someone listening rather than looking. That's true of any automated accessibility tool, not a shortcoming specific to one. A flagged item means "here's something worth a look," not "something is broken."
